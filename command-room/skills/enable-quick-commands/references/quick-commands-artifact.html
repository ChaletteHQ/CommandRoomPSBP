What arrives on its own
(standard times — yours may differ)
  • 7:00 AM your morning brief — calendar, overnight email, today's meetings prepped
  • 7:15 AM inbox pass, drafts staged
  • 8:30 AM follow-ups worth chasing
  • 5:00 PM the close: how the day went, what slipped, tomorrow proposed
  • Friday 1 PM the week wrapped
  • Sunday evening quiet upkeep: tidying, memory checks, insights
  • Through the day, silently — meetings captured as they finish, sent email reconciled against promises, so the 5 PM close verifies instead of scrambling.
None of this needs starting. It shows up. Your job is what you do with it.

All of these times are yours to move — just say it: "move my morning brief to 6:30", "no close on Fridays", "make the week wrap Monday morning." Plain language, changed once, sticks.

What the buttons do
  • Rows on this card — click one, it types the phrase into chat for you. That's all they are.
  • Numbered items in a brief or close — reply with the number (or tap it) to confirm, complete, or pick. "2" is a full answer.
  • Run Now on a scheduled task — runs it early; the schedule carries on unchanged.
  • Update notes after an upgrade — announcements, not requests. Nothing to approve; they're telling you what got better.

There is no command list.

Everything above is a shortcut, not the surface. Command Room sits on your email, calendar, meetings, and every promise on file — so the real skill is asking real questions.

  1. Ride the rhythm — the brief and the close come to you; your job is the confirms.
  2. Talk normally — "I'm worried about the Jenkins renewal" works. No syntax.
  3. Correct in passing — "actually, that's done" / "never open with pleasantries." Said once, remembered everywhere.
  4. New chats are free — the files hold the memory, the conversation never did. Start fresh anytime.
  5. Nothing writes without you — every capture confirmable, every change undoable, "what did you change" always answers.
From real client use cases
  1. A struggling direct report, review coming up: asked for a PIP — got one grounded in the actual record, commitments made, met, and missed, meeting by meeting. Not a template. Their history.
  2. The night before a hard conversation with a team member: "how should I approach this with her?" — a coaching strategy built from how that person actually communicates across months of meetings.
  3. Strategic planning weekend: "what does the last quarter say we should double down on?" — a plan that argued from his own meetings and commitments, not a framework.
  4. Messy 30-email thread, promises buried everywhere: forwarded it — "scan this for commitments." His words afterward: "I freaking love the commitment thing — it enables me to not lose sight of this stuff."
  5. Back from a week out, Monday morning: "what am I walking into this week?" No command. It just knew.
  6. Annoyed once by chirpy openers: "never open with pleasantries." Typed it once. Never again.
None of these needed a special command. Think of something you wish someone would just handle — and ask, in your own words.
